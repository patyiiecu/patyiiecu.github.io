Lesson 35, Estimating a Population Proportion.

If \(X\) is a random variable, then its values may be concentrated near a point \(x_0\) (e.g., the point could be the mean \(x_0=\mu_X\)), and if we draw an interval \((x_0-r,x_0+r)\) large enough with center \(x_0\) and radius \(r\), then we may be "confident" that most of the observed values of \(X\) are covered by the interval \((x_0-r,x_0+r)\). For example, if \(X\) is normally distributed with mean \(\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\), then we can be "95% confident" that the observed values of \(X\) lie in the interval \((\mu-2\sigma,\mu+2\sigma)\). In the previous notation, we can take the center of the interval to be \(x_0=\mu\) and its radius \(r=2\sigma\).

We call the center of the above type interval a point estimate of the statistic \(X\) and an interval \((x_0-r,x_0+r)\) an interval estimate of \(X\). An interval estimate tells us a little more about the distribution of \(X\), than just a point estimate.

A confidence interval for an unknown parameter consists of an interval of
numbers based on a point estimate.  The level of confidence represents the
expected proportion of intervals that will contain the parameter if a large
number of different samples is obtained. The level of confidence is denoted by
\((1-\alpha)\cdot100\%\).






For example, with \(\alpha=0.05=5\%\) a 95% confidence interval for a normal
random variable with mean \(\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\)
is \((\mu-1.96\sigma,\mu+1.96\sigma)\).






For the same normal random variable a \((1-\alpha)\cdot100\%\) confidence
interval is \((\mu-z_{\alpha/2}\sigma,\mu+z_{\alpha/2}\sigma)\), where
the so-called critical value \(z_{\alpha}\) was mentioned earlier and
is given by z score that cuts off a right tail from the standard normal
distribution of size (or area or probability) \(\alpha\).






For example, a 95% level of confidence (\(\alpha = 0.05\)) implies that if
100 different confidence intervals are constructed, each based on a different
sample from the same population, then we will expect 95 of the intervals to
include the parameter and 5 not to include the parameter.  Confidence interval
estimates for a quantity such as the population proportion are of the form
\(\textrm{point estimate} \pm \textrm{margin of error}\).






In the above example \((\mu-1.96\sigma,\mu+1.96\sigma)\) for the normal
distribution, the point estimate is the mean \(\mu\) and the margin of
error is \(1.96\sigma\).

Confidence interval for a sample proportion.
Consider the sample proportion \(\hat p=\frac{x}{n}\) of a sample
of size \(n\).  Assume as before that number of sample points
\(n\) satisfies that \(n\le0.05N\), i.e., it is at most 0.05%
of the population size \(N\), the variance \(np(1-p)\) of \(x\) is
large enough, i.e., \(np(1-p)\ge10\).  Under these assumptions
the distribution of \(\hat p\) can be taken to be normal with mean
\(\mu_{\hat p}=p\) and standard deviation
\(\sigma_{\hat p}=\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}\).
The point estimate is \(p\) and the margin of error is
\(1.96\sigma_{\hat p}\).
Thus the 95% confidence interval for the sample proportion \(\hat p\) is
\((p-1.96\sigma_{\hat p},p+1.96\sigma_{\hat p})\).






An obvious difficulty in using the foregoing formulas is that we usually do not
know the value of \(p\), which is the quantity that we set out to estimate
via sampling in the first place.  In practice then we can replace the unknown
value of \(p=\mu_{\hat p}\) by the sample mean \(\hat p\), and the
standard deviation \(\sigma_{\hat p}=\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}\) by
\(\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n}}\), i.e., we simply replace the unknown
\(p\) by the known \(\hat p\) in the formulas.  Therefore, the 95%
confidence interval for the sample proportion \(\hat p\) is \(\Big(\hat
p-1.96\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n}},\hat p+1.96\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat
p)}{n}}\Big)\).






Similarly, the theoretical form of a \((1-\alpha)\cdot100\%\) confidence
interval for the sample proportion \(\hat p\) is
\(\Big(p-z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}},p+z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}\Big)\).
The practical form of the same type of confidence interval is
\(\Big(\hat p-z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n}},\hat p+z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n}}\Big)\), where we simply replace the unknown
\(p\) by the known \(\hat p\) in the formulas.
We must remember that this formula requires the assumptions that
\(n\le0.05N\) and \(n\hat p(1-\hat p)\ge10\).
The radius of the confidence interval is the margin of error and is given by
\(E=z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}\) in the theoretical form and by
\(E=z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n}}\) in the practical form.






How large a sample size do we need in order to get a prescribed margin of error
\(E\) or less for a \((1-\alpha)\cdot100\%\) confidence interval?  If we
rearrange the equation \(E=z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n}}\)
for \(n\), then we get \(n=\hat p(1-\hat p)(\frac{z_{\alpha/2}}{E})^2\);
as the sample size \(n\) has to be an integer, we just round up the above
value to the nearest integer that is larger: \(n=\lceil\hat p(1-\hat
p)(\frac{z_{\alpha/2}}{E})^2\rceil\).  When we need to make a decision as to the
value of the sample size, we may not have the sample proportion \(\hat p\)
in hand (since we determine the sample proportion after the survey of the
sample has been completed).  We can make an informed guess or conduct a smaller
sample to get an approximate value of \(\hat p\) to put into the above
formula.  As a conservative estimate, we can upper estimate the product
\(\hat p(1-\hat p)\) as \(\hat p(1-\hat p)\le1/4\), which can be seen by
maximizing the upside down parabola \(x(1-x)\) for \(0\le x\le 1\).
So the conservative (or largest) sample size that is needed for a margin
of error \(E\) in a \((1-\alpha)\cdot100\%\) confidence interval is
\(n=\lceil0.25(\frac{z_{\alpha/2}}{E})^2\rceil\), i.e., we round up to the
nearest larger integer.

We make five experiments in R.
In each experiment we generate a million random numbers \(x\) drawn from
binom(n=100,p=0.3), form \(\hat p=x/n\), estimate \(p\) by the mean of
the copies of \(\hat p\), make a 95% confidence interval for each value of

Question. Given the confidence interval \((0.201,0.249)\) and the
sample size \(n=1200\), compute the point estimate of the population
proportion \(\hat p\), the margin of error \(E\), and the number \(x\)
of points in the sample that have the "specified property" (of which
the proportion is being determined).







The point estimate is the center of the confidence interval:
\(0.5(0.201+0.249)=0.225\).  The margin of error is the radius of the
confidence interval: \(E=0.5(0.249-0.201)=0.024\).  The number of guys that
like spinach (i.e., satisfy the "specified property") is the point estimate of
\(\hat p\) times the sample size \(n\), possibly rounded:
\(x=0.225\times1200=270\).
